# What This Study Contributes, and What It Does Not

**Purpose:** the Step 1 to 8 results are in; this page says which of them carry the study's claim, which are known results restated in a small environment, and what the claim should be when the report and any paper are written. It is the positioning the [Phase 1 report outline](phase1-report-outline.md) section 8 and the [November 2 agenda](meetings/2026-11-02-advisor-meeting-agenda.md) section 2 rest on.
**Sources:** the [Step 2 analysis](step2-failure-analysis.md), the [Step 3](step3-results.md), [Step 3b](step3b-results.md), [Step 3c](step3c-terminal-check.md), [Step 4](step4-results.md), [Step 5](step5-results.md), [Step 6](step6-results.md), [Step 7](step7-results.md) and [Step 8](step8-results.md) results, and the [literature reviews](lit-reviews/README.md).

## 1. The claim in one sentence

An opponent-specific weakness of a self-play agent can be *tactical* rather than *strategic*: a short, exactly calculable sequence of moves, not a misjudgement of the position. For that kind, the value head cannot be taught the lesson by any of three routes tried, more diverse training opponents, the value target at the position the search reads, or the exact answer resolved inside the training search, and the reason can be counted in the training data; a cheap exact search removes the loss outright; and the policy head can be taught it, partly, by one route only: a search run at the position the search reads, which gives it a target there. What the policy head carries grows with the weight of that signal in its loss, whether the weight comes from more such positions or from counting the same positions more times: first the positions of the opponents that produce them, then, once the signal is doubled, the boards of a knowledge-free opponent that finds the same hole elsewhere, a move read in the pooled series of checkpoints and not on any one of them. The gain is concave in the signal, the positions limit themselves because each lesson teaches the network to stop producing the next, and raising the signal by playing more games against the opponents is past a point paid for with the network's own strength, because the agent is by then mostly not training on its own play and has stopped being the kind of agent the claim is about; raising it by a weight in the loss, with the games unchanged, reaches the same place and pays nothing, up to a weight of 8 at which the opponent's rows are two fifths of the policy loss. Past a weighted count of about 300 the signal keeps buying the positions of the opponents that produce it and no more of the knowledge-free opponent's boards, and twice the distinct positions at the same weighted count buy no more of them either, read on three seeds. Where that row stops under the frozen protocol is where a 50-simulation search stops on the priors these networks learned: the same networks lose a quarter as many of those games at 256 simulations a move and none at 800, while the untrained network at 800 still loses as many of them as the exact check leaves at 50. The exact search reaches every board at once and for nothing.

## 2. What is not new

Three things in these results are known, and the report should say so before a reader does.

- **A one-move look-ahead for game-ending replies.** Resolving what can be resolved exactly before asking an evaluator is standard practice in classical game programs, and AlphaZero-style search also scores a finished position exactly when it reaches one. The Step 3c rule reaches it one move earlier. A result whose headline is "a terminal check raised the score against Greedy from 67.5% to 94.0%" has no claim in it.
- **Self-play agents can be beaten by simple policies.** Wang et al. (2023) showed it against KataGo at superhuman strength, with a trained adversary. The Step 1 and 2 results are the same phenomenon in an environment small enough to take apart, which is their use, not their novelty.
- **Self-play overfits to itself.** Lanctot et al. (2017) named it; Czempin and Gleave (2022) proposed population-based training as the remedy. That the training distribution is the agent's own is the starting point here, not a finding.

## 3. What is new

Four things, in the order of how much weight they can carry.

**A causal chain measured at every link, not inferred at any.** Step 1 reproduced the pattern under a frozen protocol and found that every loss to Greedy is a wipeout. Step 2 located it in the value head (one disc rated a near-certain win) and in the prior (the wiping reply at 4%), and showed with a knowledge-free held-out opponent that the hole is not Greedy's. Step 3 tested the data-side remedy the literature points to, opponent exposure, with pre-registered predictions, and it failed. Step 3b tested the one-variable repair Step 3 proposed, training the value head exactly where the search reads it, and it failed too, and the training rows were counted to say why. Step 3c tested the search-side alternative, found it works, and read the residue. Step 4 put the exact answer inside the training search itself and found it removes the lesson rows rather than adding them. Step 5 gave the *policy* head a target at the position the search reads, and the prior that Step 2 had named as the gate doubled, the plain search began to escape, and the escapes were traced to the prior with a policy-only search. Step 6 ran the dose curve on those rows and found the held-out row moves once they are doubled, in the pooled series and on no single checkpoint, which withdrew the ceiling Step 5 had read and bounded the route by its cost instead. Step 7 reproduced the curve with the games held fixed, by counting the rows two and four times in the policy loss, and found the same readings at no cost, which located the mechanism in the policy signal and the cost in the displaced self-play. Step 8 pushed the weight to 8 and found the Greedy family still falling, nothing paid, and the held-out row where it was, so the signal is not what stops that row; it combined the two axes and found the held-out row at the weight-4 reading on two seeds of three, the third being the lowest reading in the series, so distinct positions are not what stops that row either: under the frozen protocol it stops at the same place however the policy signal is bought. Most exploitability work stops at "the hole exists and an adversary can be trained to find it". Following one hole from the game record to the training rows and back, testing each repair by elimination, and ending with the one that moves the quantity the analysis named, is the part no prior work on this question has done.

**A countable reason why value regression cannot learn a tactical exception.** Step 3b, section 5: in twenty generations of training with the opponent's positions recorded, the trainer saw between about 80 and 170 rows carrying the lesson (few discs, opponent to move, opponent won; the count depends on where the disc and ply filters are drawn, Step 3b results, section 5) and more than four thousand rows that look the same to a value head and say the opposite, because a side with few discs in the middle of an Othello game usually does win. The value head learned the average, and the average is right. This is not a fact about Othello. Any evaluator trained by regression on game results will learn the typical outcome of a state and miss an exception that depends on one reply, when the exception is outnumbered twenty to one by states it is assumed unable to tell apart. That assumption is what the counts support; no run has tested it (section 5 below). The count makes the failure quantitative: it says how rare the lesson was relative to its look-alikes, and it predicts (section 5 below) when the same failure will recur.

**A split in the literature's remedy.** Opponent diversity is recommended against opponent-specific weakness in general. Step 3 shows what it does and does not do here: it put nine thousand low-disc positions into a training window where self-play had put fifty, so it reached the state; the lesson inside the state was not learnable by value regression, so it did not take. The recommendation is right for weaknesses of judgement, where seeing more positions of a kind changes the estimate of that kind. It does not reach weaknesses of calculation, where the estimate of the kind is already right and the loss turns on one move. Stating the limit of a remedy, with the experiment that finds it, is worth more than one more case where the remedy worked.

**Which head, and at which position.** The same games, recorded three ways, teach three different things. Recording only the network's own positions (Step 3) moves the value head at those positions and nowhere the search reads. Recording the opponent's positions as value-only rows (Step 3b) moves nothing: the value target there is an average the look-alikes own. Searching the opponent's positions and recording the policy target (Step 5) moves the prior on the wiping reply from 4% to 8% at positions never seen, cuts the wipeouts against every disc-hungry opponent by a third on both seeds, and costs nothing in strength, calibration or retention, where the one earlier route that cut them (a material term in the value target, Step 3 group D) paid with half the agent's strength. Doubling and quadrupling the searched rows (Step 6) took the prior to 10% and 12%, moved the held-out random-playout row by a third in the pooled series, with every new checkpoint at or below 27 where the runs without searched rows spread from 21 to 39, and at four times the rows cost 11 to 20 points on the retention and Edax rows, because the network's own play had become a fifth of its data. Counting the Step 5 rows two and four times in the policy loss instead (Step 7) reached the same prior, the same family and the same held-out reading with the games unchanged and nothing paid, and eight times (Step 8) took the family lower still, at no cost, without moving the held-out row. The difference between a null and a result was not the data but which head was given a target at which ply, and how much of its loss that target was. That is a statement about where a learned evaluator and a learned prior each can and cannot carry a one-move fact, and it is the part of the chain that a reader can act on.

**The method.** A frozen evaluation protocol audited before any experiment; a held-out roster with a written admission rule and a measured similarity check; a held-out opponent with no learned or hand-written knowledge that reproduces the weakness independently; eight experiments with predictions written before the runs, three of which failed and are reported as such, two pre-registered thresholds in the fifth missed by a little and reported as missed, the sixth's claim-side prediction failing in the direction its design said would change the claim, which it did, the seventh reproducing the sixth's curve with the games held fixed, which located its mechanism, and the eighth's combined group reading outside every outcome its design wrote on two seeds, reported as that rather than as the nearest one and settled by a third seed run for the purpose; and one audit of the series' own wording, which found the held-out row read against too narrow a reference and corrected it. For a one-term undergraduate study this is the standard a reader will notice first.

## 4. Tactical and strategic weaknesses, and the horizon between them

The words come from chess, where they are used the way they are used here.

A **tactical** weakness is a loss that turns on a short, concrete sequence of moves whose outcome can be calculated exactly: here, one reply that takes every disc and ends the game. The position before it is not misjudged in general; it is misjudged in one specific line. Calculation finds the line, and finds it faster than any amount of learned judgement, because the line's outcome is a fact about the rules.

A **strategic** weakness is a misjudgement of what a position is worth that no calculation of affordable length corrects: overvaluing material, undervaluing mobility or corners, trading the wrong thing for the wrong thing over forty moves. The loss arrives slowly, there is no single refuting line to find, and a deeper search helps only in proportion to how much of the long game it can see. Improving judgement means improving the evaluator, which means learning.

The two are not a binary. What separates them is the **horizon**: how many moves an exact calculation has to read before it can confirm the outcome without asking the evaluator. The wipeout here has a horizon of one move. A capturing race in Go has a horizon of the whole race, often tens of moves, but it is still a calculation: counting liberties and reading out the fill is something a beginner can do exactly, and classical Go programs did it with dedicated life-and-death and capturing-race readers rather than with their general evaluation. A positional misjudgement has no horizon at which calculation settles it.

Where a weakness sits on that axis decides what fixes it, and the fix differs from one end to the other.

| | horizon of one move (here) | horizon of a long forced line | no finite horizon |
|---|---|---|---|
| what is wrong | one calculable reply is never looked at | one calculable line is never read to the end, and the evaluator misjudges every position along it | the estimate of a kind of position is off |
| what fixes it | exact resolution at the node (Step 3c) | a dedicated exact reader for that kind of line, or a search budget that reaches the end despite the evaluator | a better evaluator: more or different data, a different target |
| what opponent diversity does | reaches the state, cannot teach the reply (Steps 3, 3b) | teaches the evaluator about such lines, if enough of them with enough variety are seen; the adversary then finds the next one | teaches the kind, if the new opponents produce it |
| what the value target can carry | not the exception, when its look-alikes outnumber it (Step 3b, section 5); not even when the training search resolves it exactly, which removes the exception from the data (Step 4) | the average over such positions, which is right most of the time and wrong in the race | the shift in the average, which is the point |
| what the policy target can carry | the reply, when a search is run where the search reads: first at positions like the ones searched (Step 5: prior 4% to 8%, the Greedy family's wipeouts down a third, the knowledge-free opponent's unmoved), then on the knowledge-free opponent's different boards once the searched rows are doubled (Step 6: prior to 12%, its row down a third in the pooled series), or counted twice in the loss (Step 7: the same readings, the games unchanged); concave in the signal, and the positions limit themselves | the first move of the line, where a search found it; the rest of the line is beyond what one target teaches | the kind, as the value target does, since both are averages over positions |
| cost of the fix | microseconds a position, no network calls | a solver per tactic, or search that scales with the line's length | GPU hours, and a calibration or strength cost elsewhere (Step 3 group D) |

Steps 5 and 6 read the first column from the other side. A learned prior is a memory of positions that generalises as far as its data reaches. At 130 searched fatal rows it reached the positions the Greedy family produces and not the ones the random-playout search produces, which attack the same one-ply fact on different boards; at 200 to 320 rows it reached those too, by a third over the pooled checkpoints, with the gain per row falling and, at the larger dose, the network's strength falling with it. Step 7 says what the memory is a memory of: 110 of those positions counted twice in the loss reached the knowledge-free opponent's boards as far as 200 counted once, and 85 counted four times closed the prior's tail as far as 320 counted once, so the quantity the prior generalises in proportion to is the weight of the search's signal at such positions, not their number, and the games the dose had to be paid in were never the active ingredient. Step 8 says where that stops: 80 positions counted eight times took the family lower than any group and the held-out row no lower, so past a weighted count of about 300 the signal reaches the positions it was drawn from and no more of the knowledge-free opponent's, and 170 distinct positions counted twice reach no more of them than 85 counted four times, on three seeds. The exact check reaches every board at once, because it reads the board rather than remembering it. That is the practical content of "tactical": the fix that calculates generalises for nothing; the fix that learns generalises in proportion to what it is shown, and what it can be shown is bounded by the game itself, since each lesson teaches the network to stop producing the next.

The test that places a weakness on the axis is the one Step 2 and Step 3c applied: leave the learned evaluator alone, add exact calculation of a chosen depth, and ask whether the loss goes. The Step 2 budget series (50, 256, 800 simulations: wipeouts fall by a third to a half per step and never to zero) looked, on its own, like a long horizon or none. The one-ply check at 50 simulations, which removes 82 to 88% of the wipeouts and more than 256 simulations do, says the search was not short of depth in general but of one specific ply that the prior kept it from spending. The full curve ([Step 3c, section 7](step3c-terminal-check.md#7-the-search-budget-curve)) says the same with four budgets and four networks: on the untrained control 800 simulations leave no fewer wipeouts than the check leaves at 50, and on the networks trained by the policy route the plain search reaches the check's reading at about 256, because the prior they learned is what a modest budget needs to find the reply. The training route and the search budget multiply, and the exact rule makes both unnecessary for this tactic.

The cyclic attack on KataGo (Wang et al. 2023) reads as the middle column. The decisive error is one move, failing to start filling liberties in a race the network has misjudged, and the correct move is exactly calculable, so the weakness is tactical in kind. But the horizon is the length of the race, the value network misjudges every position along it, and the policy network does not propose the fill, so search receives no help from either head: the adversary still wins 72% of games against KataGo at ten million visits. The weakness here is the same kind at the shortest possible horizon, which is why one cheap rule removes it and why the result can be placed by experiment. The general statement both support: a learned evaluator with a generic search inherits the tactical blind spots that classical programs covered with dedicated exact readers, and the cost of recovering each one by search alone grows with its horizon. The terminal check is the smallest such reader.

## 5. What would make the claim stronger

In order of cost.

- **A second knowledge-free opponent**, so that the transfer result rests on more than one row.
- **The lesson-to-look-alike ratio as a predictor.** The replay data of every Step 3 and 3b run are on the cluster. For each run, count the rows that carry the wipeout lesson and the rows that match them on everything a value head can see, and set the ratio against whether that run's value head moved at the fatal position (the probe says it did not, for every run). One ratio and one outcome per run is a weak test with seven runs; the stronger form varies the ratio on purpose, by weighting the lesson rows in the loss, and predicts the value head moves once the ratio crosses a threshold. This turns the count in Step 3b from an explanation into a measurable quantity.
- **A second tactic, or a second game.** One wipeout rule in one game is one example. A second one-move tactic with the same signature (a usually favourable state, one refuting reply, a lesson outnumbered in the data) in 6×6 Othello or another game would make section 3's second point a regularity instead of an observation. This is the Phase 2 item that most changes what the claim can be.

## 6. What this means for the report

- The headline is not the 94%, and it is not the 45 either. It is the sentence in section 1, with the chain in section 3 as its evidence and the split in section 4 as its interpretation.
- The three nulls are results, not setbacks: each excludes a route into the value head, and the exclusions are what make the one route into the policy head readable as a located fact rather than a lucky recipe.
- The Step 5 to 8 results are reported with their limits in the same sentence as their size: a third fewer wipeouts against the opponents that produce the trained positions at the first dose, a third fewer against the knowledge-free opponent at the second, read pooled over checkpoints because one checkpoint does not resolve it, the gain concave and self-limiting, the largest dose paid for with a fifth of the network's strength, and the same gain for nothing when the signal is raised by a weight in the loss instead of by games, up to a weight of 8, where the family keeps falling and the held-out row does not.
- The discussion should state the limits plainly: one game, one tactic, one starting checkpoint, two seeds per training group and three in one, no adaptive adversary, and a repair that is standard practice whose contribution is where it was shown to be the only working one, not that it works.
